Engineering Economics is a course that teaches students how to evaluate engineering projects and make financially sound decisions. It combines engineering analysis with economic principles.
Mathematical Foundations of Linear Spatial Systems

======

Typical topics include:

Time value of money: Present value, future value, annual worth, and cash-flow diagrams
Interest rates: Simple and compound interest, nominal and effective rates
Project evaluation: Present Worth (NPV), Annual Worth, Future Worth, and Rate of Return (IRR)
Alternative comparison: Choosing between competing machines, designs, technologies, or projects
Depreciation and taxes: Book value, depreciation methods, taxable income, and after-tax cash flows
Break-even and sensitivity analysis: Studying how changes in cost, revenue, interest rate, or project life affect a decision
Replacement analysis: Determining when existing equipment should be replaced
Inflation and escalation: Adjusting economic analysis for changing prices
Risk and uncertainty: Evaluating projects when future costs and benefits are not certain
The central question of the course is:

Given several technically feasible alternatives, which option provides the best economic result under the relevant constraints?

For example, an engineer may need to compare two machines:

Machine A costs less initially but has higher operating costs.
Machine B costs more initially but lasts longer and requires less maintenance.
Engineering economics provides the methods needed to compare these alternatives fairly over time.

The course is usually quantitative, but understanding the assumptions behind each method is as important as performing the calculations. A strong answer should normally include the cash-flow diagram, the selected economic method, the calculation steps, and a clear recommendation.
